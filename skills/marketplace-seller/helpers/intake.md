# Intake

The grilling phase. Runs first, before photos and before any price. Its job is to leave nothing unknown that would change the price, the copy, or the safety of the handoff — and to prove it by getting the human to confirm the picture back.

Sellers underspecify by default. Not evasively: an item is obvious to the person holding it, so they describe the half a stranger already knows and skip the half that sets the price.

## What to ask

**Ask only what changes the output.** Every question must alter a price, a line of copy, a structured field, or a safety call. A question that changes nothing costs the human patience they will need later.

Three things are never worth asking:

- **What the photos will answer.** Colour, visible wear, what's in the box. Wait and look.
- **What you can find yourself.** Launch price, retail price, specs, release year — look them up.
- **What you already know.** Anything in the item record, or said earlier in the conversation.

## How to ask

**Batch the round.** Ask everything answerable now in one numbered list, not one at a time. The human is listing several items; a six-message interrogation per item is how a throughput tool becomes a chore.

**Give your best guess with each question**, so the fast path is agreeing rather than composing.

> 3. Is it unlocked, or tied to a network? — I'd assume unlocked unless you bought it on a plan.

**Hold back what depends on an answer.** A question whose sense depends on one still open belongs in the next round, not this one. Most items need one round; two is normal when the first answer opens something up.

**Stop when the remaining unknowns cannot change anything.** Not when the list is exhausted — when nothing left would move the price, the copy, or the safety call.

## Every item

1. **Exact variant** — the specific model, storage, size, or trim. Buyers filter on it and comps depend on it.
2. **Age** — when they got it, and whether new or secondhand.
3. **Cost** — what they paid. `0` if it was already theirs.
4. **What's included** — and what is missing. Box, charger, cables, receipt, spares.
5. **Faults** — anything that does not work as intended, beyond cosmetic wear.
6. **Reason for selling** — buyers ask on nearly every used listing, and dodging reads exactly like the pattern Facebook teaches them to distrust.
7. **How many** — one, or multiple units.

## By category

**Phones, tablets, laptops** — battery health or cycle count; carrier-locked or open; screen or battery ever replaced; accounts signed out yet; any repair history.

**Shoes and clothing** — size and the sizing standard (US/UK/EU); original box; worn how often; any stretch, pilling, or odour; authenticity proof for branded items.

**Furniture** — dimensions; material; disassembles or not; who moves it and up how many flights; smoke or pets in the home.

**Appliances** — working, and tested how recently; age; warranty remaining; voltage; who uninstalls it.

**Bags and watches** — authenticity proof; serial or date code; servicing history; dust bag, box, papers.

**Anything electrical** — tested working, and how recently. "It worked last time I used it" is not tested.

## Close the loop

Before pricing, hand the picture back in a short block: what it is, condition as described, what's included, what's missing, faults, cost, quantity, and where they are.

Then ask them to confirm or correct it. Two reasons this earns its line — the human catches what they forgot when they see it stated plainly, and a fact confirmed here is one you can write into copy without hedging later.

Mark clearly anything still unconfirmed, especially condition. Their claim is a claim until the photos agree: `photos.md` grades it, this file does not.

Once confirmed, write the record and move to `photos.md`.
